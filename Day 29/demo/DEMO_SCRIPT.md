## 🎬 Step-by-Step Live Execution

### Step 1: In-Domain Query — Category: Bias (0:30 - 1:15)

**Presenter Says:**  
> *"To begin our live demo, let's query the system about a real-world machine learning bias incident. I will execute `python app.py` with a natural language inquiry about resume screening bias."*

**Command to Run:**
```bash
python app.py "How did AI show bias in hiring resumes?"
```

**Real Expected Output:**
```text
================================================================
QUERY: How did AI show bias in hiring resumes?
CONFIDENCE: 15.65% (Threshold: 15.00%)
================================================================

ANSWER:
Regarding 'Amazon Automated Resume Screening Tool Gender Bias' (2018): Amazon developed an internal machine learning recruiting engine to automate resume screening and identify top software engineering talent. The model was trained on resumes submitted to the company over a ten-year window. Because technology industry hiring was historically male-dominated, the algorithm learned to penalize candidate profiles containing the word 'women's' (such as 'women's chess club captain') and downgraded graduates of all-women's colleges. Engineers attempted to neutralize specific gender terms, but the model discovered correlated proxy terms. Amazon ultimately scrapped the system in 2018 before deployment because equal treatment could not be guaranteed.

Key Takeaway: Training algorithms on historical hiring data codifies and amplifies historical human biases; removing obvious demographic protected attributes is insufficient when proxy variables remain.

SOURCES:
  1. Amazon Automated Resume Screening Tool Gender Bias (2018) [BIAS] - Similarity: 15.65%
================================================================
```

**Key Explanations to Deliver:**
- Point out the **Confidence: 15.65%**, which clears our calibrated threshold of 15.00%.
- Highlight that the answer identifies the specific company (Amazon), year (2018), failure mechanism (proxy terms penalizing women's colleges), and the core engineering takeaway.
- Emphasize that the source is transparently cited with its taxonomy category `[BIAS]`.

### Step 2: Query from a Different Category — Category: Hallucination (1:15 - 2:00)

**Presenter Says:**  
> *"Now let's shift categories entirely from algorithmic bias to generative hallucination. In 2023, attorneys made global headlines for submitting fabricated cases generated by an AI chatbot. Let's see how the engine handles this specific scenario."*

**Command to Run:**
```bash
python app.py "Lawyers submitting fake AI hallucinated legal cases to court"
```

**Real Expected Output:**
```text
================================================================
QUERY: Lawyers submitting fake AI hallucinated legal cases to court
CONFIDENCE: 17.48% (Threshold: 15.00%)
================================================================

ANSWER:
Regarding 'Mata v. Avianca Fictitious Case Citations in Federal Court' (2023): Attorneys representing Roberto Mata in a personal injury lawsuit against Avianca Airlines in US District Court used ChatGPT to perform legal research. The chatbot fabricated non-existent judicial decisions, bogus judicial quotes, and invalid docket numbers, including 'Varghese v. China Southern Airlines'. The attorneys submitted these hallucinated precedents in a formal federal court brief without independently verifying the citations in official law reporters. When opposing counsel and the presiding judge could not locate the cited authorities, the court uncovered the hallucination and sanctioned the attorneys with monetary fines and professional reprimand.

Key Takeaway: Large language models generate plausible-sounding syntactic hallucinations rather than verified factual truth; professional workflows require mandatory human verification against authoritative primary sources.

SOURCES:
  1. Mata v. Avianca Fictitious Case Citations in Federal Court (2023) [HALLUCINATION] - Similarity: 17.48%
================================================================
```

**Key Explanations to Deliver:**
- Point out the higher **Confidence of 17.48%**.
- Note the retrieval of the exact federal court precedent: *Mata v. Avianca (2023)*.
- Show that the lesson reinforces our engineering objective: generative systems must be checked against deterministic ground truth.

### Step 3: Out-of-Domain Guardrail Refusal (2:00 - 2:45)

**Presenter Says:**  
> *"One of the greatest dangers in production RAG systems is hallucination when asked questions outside their knowledge base. Most naive systems will retrieve the nearest arbitrary document and force an answer. Let's watch how our calibrated guardrail responds when we ask an completely unrelated question."*

**Command to Run:**
```bash
python app.py "What is the recipe for chocolate cake?"
```

**Real Expected Output:**
```text
================================================================
QUERY: What is the recipe for chocolate cake?
CONFIDENCE: 0.00% (Threshold: 15.00%)
================================================================

I don't have enough information to answer that.

[Guardrail Active] Query similarity was below confidence threshold.
================================================================
```

**Key Explanations to Deliver:**
- Point out that the **Cosine Similarity was 0.00%**, falling well beneath our 15.00% cutoff.
- The guardrail immediately prevented generation and returned: *"I don't have enough information to answer that."*
- Notice that zero sources were attributed, preventing false attribution.
